Interviewer Training Guidance
Before conducting interviews, interviewers must be trained on the precise meaning of each problem variable and how to identify problematic responses. This training is critical for maintaining data quality.

Understanding Problem Framework Variables
Each interviewer must understand what each variable IS and what it is NOT:
Solution
IS:
• The product, service, or offering that the startup provides
• What the startup has built and is selling/offering to customers
• Example: "AI-powered brand monitoring dashboard" or "Automated expense management platform" or "No-code workflow automation tool"
IS NOT:
• Vague marketing taglines (e.g., "we help companies succeed")
• Benefits or outcomes (those are goals/aspirations)
• Customer problems or pain points (those are barriers)
• Aspirational products not yet built or publicly launched

Customer Segment
IS:
• Those whose pain points the startup is alleviating, as represented in their public communication
• Example: "HR managers at 50-200 person companies"
IS NOT:
• End users who may differ from the target audience that the startup appeals to on their website (e.g., kids using app vs. parents who are the target)
• Aspirational customers not yet publicly targeted (violates the public info requirement) 

Identifying the Real Customer (Multi-Stakeholder Framework)
When multiple stakeholders are involved (e.g., CFO pays, engineers use), determine the PRIMARY customer:
DECISION FRAMEWORK: Who is the Startup Solving For?
1. Website Value Proposition Test
• Read the homepage headline and key benefits
• Whose problem is being emphasized?
• ✓ "Cut infrastructure costs by 40%" → CFO/Finance/Organization 
• ✓ "Deploy faster with zero-downtime migrations" → Engineering
2. Case Study/Testimonial Test
• Who is quoted in testimonials and case studies?
• What role/title appears most frequently?
• ✓ If testimonials are from VPs of Engineering → Engineering is customer
3. Barriers & Goals Alignment Test
• Review the Goals and Barriers from the website/public materials
• From whose perspective are these written?
• ✓ "Barrier: Manual approval workflows slow procurement" → Operations
• ✓ "Goal: Give developers self-service access" → Engineering
4. Jobs-to-be-Done Anchor
• Ask: "When someone 'hires' this product, whose job is getting done?"
• The CFO may approve budget, but whose day-to-day problem is solved?

Multi-Stakeholder Examples
Example 1: Developer Tool Bought by CFO
• Product: CI/CD platform
• CFO: Approves budget, cares about cost efficiency
• Engineering: Uses daily, cares about deployment speed
• CUSTOMER: Engineering team (their pain points drive the purchase)
• Rationale: Website emphasizes developer productivity, testimonials from CTOs/VPs of Eng

Example 2: Financial Software Bought by CFO
• Product: Expense management platform
• CFO: Approves AND uses for reporting/compliance
• Employees: Submit expenses
• CUSTOMER: CFO/Finance team (their pain points drive the purchase)
• Rationale: Website emphasizes financial controls, audit compliance, cost visibility

Example 3: Learning Platform (Complex)
• Product: Corporate training LMS
• HR: Buys it, assigns courses
• Employees: Take courses
• Managers: Track completion
• CUSTOMER: HR (their pain points around compliance, onboarding drive purchase)
• Rationale: Website emphasizes "simplify compliance training," "onboard faster," testimonials from CHROs

RULE OF THUMB
If the website could be rewritten to appeal to a different stakeholder without changing the product, you've identified the wrong customer.
The customer is whoever experiences the core barriers and goals the startup emphasizes publicly. As a validation, apply the DECISION FRAMEWORK described above,
 
Context (When Does the Problem Occur)
IS:
• The specific triggering situation or timing when the problem occurs (e.g., "when preparing quarterly business reviews")
IS NOT:
• General business operations, vague timing like "always" or "whenever working"

Goal/Aspiration
IS:
• The desired outcome or end state the customer wants to achieve - independent of any specific product, feature, or technology (e.g., "track brand visibility in AI search results" )
• Goal/Aspiration is (1) Future-oriented (describes what “better” looks like from the customer’s perspective) (2) solution-agnostic (focuses on progress, not on the product they might use) (3) emotional / functional (can capture deeper desires such as feelings and emotions)

IS NOT:
• Features of your solution, process steps, generic statements like "be more efficient"

Barriers
IS:
• Specific obstacles preventing desired goal or aspiration (e.g., for the aspiration “Evaluate startup investment opportunities quickly and confidently.” One barrier is “Hard to gather reliable, comparable data on early-stage startups.”)
• Barriers explain why customers are stuck with imperfect solutions. Startups offer solutions that attempt to eliminate these barriers and allow customers to progress. 
IS NOT:
• Vague terms like "lack of resources," "complexity," competitive disadvantages
 
Critical Filtering Rules: Nonsense Responses, Private vs. Public Information
IMPORTANT: Ensure Consistency with Public Communication
Interview Technique:
Prompt founders with: "Think about your website first" to anchor responses in publicly available information.
Primary Focus:
Verify consistency of solutions and customer segments with what the startup publicly communicates.
Filter TWO Types of Problematic Responses:
1. Private Information: Internal strategy, aspirational products not yet public, customer details learned only through sales
2. Nonsensical Responses: Founder comments that are incomprehensible, contradictory, or unrelated to the variable being discussed
Note: Barriers and aspirations are never explicitly mentioned online, but different products or customer segments may imply different barriers/aspirations.
Identifying Nonsensical Responses
RED FLAGS During Interviews
General Red Flags:
• Unrelated to variable: Founder discusses barriers when asked about goals, or vice versa
• Contradictory statements: Founder says something doesn't apply, then provides details about it
• Incomprehensible reasoning: Explanation makes no logical sense or cannot be understood by interviewer
• Off-website divergence: Describes customers or products dramatically different from website without clear explanation
Customer Segment Red Flags:
• Founder describes a customer that doesn't match ANY website messaging (e.g., company name or brand doesn’t match website)
• Founder lists 3+ equally important customer segments (website should have primary focus)
• Founder says "everyone needs this" without specificity
• Customer segment changes when discussing different variables (inconsistency signal)
ACTION:
When you detect nonsensical responses, gently probe:
"Let me clarify - I'm asking specifically about [variable]. Can you help me understand how [their response] relates to [variable definition]?"
OR
"When I look at your website, who would I think is your primary customer? Walk me through how your homepage speaks to them."

Public vs Private Information
Before addressing corner cases, interviewers must understand the clear definitions of what qualifies as PUBLIC vs PRIVATE information.

PUBLIC Information
Information that is available to anyone without special access or relationship with the company.
Sources include:
• Company website (current version)
• LinkedIn profiles and company page
• PitchBook or Crunchbase entries
• Press releases and media coverage
• Published case studies and testimonials
• Blog posts and public presentations
• Social media posts from official company accounts
Key principle: If an LLM could find it through web search, it's PUBLIC.

PRIVATE Information
Information that requires insider access, direct relationship with the company, or privileged communication.
Examples include:
• Internal strategy: Roadmaps, go-to-market plans, pricing strategies not publicly disclosed
• Aspirational products: Features or offerings being developed but not yet announced or marketed
• Sales conversations: What founders learn about customer needs through 1-on-1 sales calls
• Customer specifics: Detailed information about specific customer implementations or pain points
• Pivot plans: Future directions or changes not yet reflected in public communication
• Metrics and financials: Performance data, customer counts, revenue figures not publicly shared
Key principle: If the information comes from being an insider, employee, customer, or through private conversations, it's PRIVATE.

Corner Cases
The following scenarios require special judgment:

Product Offering Mismatches
Startups' product offerings may not be current online, or founders may aspire to serve more products/customers than currently listed. This affects ALL variables:
• If founder describes products/features not on website → PRIVATE
• If founder describes customer segments not on website → PRIVATE
• All variables -- customer segments, contexts, aspirations, and barriers -- tied to non-public products → ALL PRIVATE
• If founder apologizes that “the website is outdated”
• Founder mentions “we used to” or “we’re pivoting to” or “we spun out from” 

Barriers and Aspirations Never Explicit Online
CRITICAL: Barriers and aspirations are NEVER explicitly stated on company websites. However, different products or customer segments naturally imply different barriers and goals.
• PUBLIC: Barriers/goals that logically follow from publicly stated products and customers
• PRIVATE: Barriers/goals tied to products or customer needs not represented on website
